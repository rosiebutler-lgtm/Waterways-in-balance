First, understand the water system

Water is always moving across the land, soaking into the ground, being stored, and flowing through waterways. Healthy forests, wetlands and soils help regulate this movement.

RainfallWater enters the ecosystem
→
RunoffWater flows over the land
↔
InfiltrationWater soaks into soil
→
StorageWater is held in soil, wetlands and waterways

Key science words

Runoff – water flowing over the land surface.

Infiltration – water entering and moving into soil.

Erosion – soil is worn away and transported by water or wind.

Nature's water helpers

  • Forests: roots hold soil, vegetation slows runoff, leaf litter protects soil and roots create pathways for water to enter.
  • Wetlands: slow water, store excess water, trap sediment and provide habitats.
  • Healthy soils: allow infiltration, store water for plants, reduce runoff and help reduce erosion.

Remember: these natural features regulate water movement — they do not stop all runoff or erosion.

How can human activity disrupt waterways?

Explore each example. Notice the chain from the human activity, to a change in water movement or quality, to an effect on living things.

Impact 1

Drought

Less water is available for plants and animals. Habitats may shrink and food sources become limited.

Water availability decreases
Impact 2

Wetland drainage

Water storage and wetland habitats are lost. Water may move downstream faster and biodiversity can decrease.

Storage and habitat are lost
Impact 3

Land clearing

Fewer roots hold soil together. Runoff and erosion can increase, carrying sediment into streams.

Runoff and erosion can increase
Impact 4

Pollution

Fertilisers, animal waste and sediment can enter waterways. Water quality declines and aquatic organisms may be harmed.

Water quality declines

Use linking words in your presentation: because · therefore · this can lead to · as a result.

Check your understanding: why might a stream become muddy after nearby trees are removed?

Fewer roots hold the soil together and there is less vegetation to slow runoff. Rain can wash more soil away; sediment is carried into the stream, making the water muddy and potentially damaging aquatic habitats.

What can people do to restore waterways?

Restoration works best when people repair damage, prevent further harm and keep caring for the ecosystem over time.

Approach 1

Restoration

  • Replant native trees and grasses beside streams.
  • Restore drained wetlands.
  • Re-establish native forest.

How it helps: roots hold soil, plants slow runoff, and wetlands store water and trap sediment.

Approach 2

Conservation

  • Protect native forests.
  • Create or maintain predator-free sanctuaries.
  • Limit development in sensitive habitats.

How it helps: protects ecosystems from further damage.

Approach 3

Long-term management

  • Monitor water quality.
  • Control invasive species.
  • Replant where vegetation has failed.

How it helps: checks progress and responds when problems remain.

Kaitiakitanga — ongoing care

Kaitiakitanga is a responsibility to care for the natural environment for current and future generations.

  • Protect waterways and freshwater habitats.
  • Restore native forests and wetlands.
  • Monitor the health of local ecosystems.
  • Work together as a community, including whānau.
  • Take ongoing responsibility — not just complete a one-off activity.

Think about a local stream, wetland or green space: who should be involved, and how could the community contribute?

Example: the Green Stream Project

A school and community group want to improve a stream running through farmland. Their proposal is to:

  • Plant native trees and grasses along stream banks.
  • Fence livestock out of the stream.
  • Restore a small wetland beside the stream.
  • Monitor water quality and native vegetation over two years.
Expected benefits

Roots help hold soil together; plants slow runoff; wetlands store water and trap sediment; fencing reduces direct livestock access and contamination; improved habitat may support greater biodiversity.

Why combine actions?

Different actions address different pressures. Monitoring over time helps the community check whether the changes are working.

Evidence lab: how do we know?

Scientists collect ecological evidence before and after restoration. No single indicator tells the whole story — use several measures over time.

Other possible influences include stream flow, soil type, rainfall, upstream land use and pollution.

Evidence

Information collected through observations or measurements.

Uncertainty

How confident we are that measurements or conclusions are accurate.

Limitations

Features of a study that restrict what we can conclude.

Make the investigation stronger

  • Measure more sites along the stream.
  • Repeat measurements over time.
  • Measure several water-quality indicators, not just clarity.
  • Record native plant survival and growth.
  • Repeat biodiversity surveys using the same sampling method.
Important: one water-clarity measurement at one location cannot show that the whole catchment has improved. Rainfall can affect clarity, and one measurement does not show a long-term trend.
Why take measurements before and after restoration?

Before measurements provide a baseline — a picture of conditions before action begins. After measurements allow scientists to compare conditions and identify changes over time. Rainfall, seasons and other changes can also influence results, so a comparison site can help scientists decide whether restoration is likely to have made a difference.

Your presentation challenge

Your role

You are an ecological advisor!

Create a presentation explaining how humans can work to restore waterways. Use the information and Green Stream evidence on this webpage to make a justified recommendation.

Suggested presentation structure

  1. Introduce the issue: explain one human activity that has changed a waterway or catchment.
  2. Explain the science: describe what happens to runoff, infiltration, water storage, erosion or water quality — then link this to biodiversity.
  3. Recommend restoration: describe at least two actions people could take and explain how each action could help.
  4. Use evidence: include at least two specific pieces of evidence from the Green Stream data.
  5. Evaluate: identify one limitation or uncertainty and explain what further evidence should be collected.
  6. Connect to kaitiakitanga: explain how people can continue caring for the waterway over time.

Teacher-style model recommendation (open after you have tried)

I recommend that the community restores the stream banks by planting native vegetation and fencing livestock out of the stream. Plant roots will help hold the soil together, reducing erosion and the amount of sediment entering the water. Vegetation can also slow runoff and help water infiltrate into the soil.

The evidence supports this action because Site A had 75% native vegetation cover and 45 cm water clarity, compared with Site C, which had only 15% vegetation cover and 12 cm water clarity. This suggests that sites with more vegetation may have healthier stream conditions.

However, only three sites were investigated, so the results may not represent the whole catchment. The community should measure water clarity, erosion and native plant cover at more sites before and after restoration to see whether conditions improve.
