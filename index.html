First, understand the water system

Water is always moving across the land, soaking into the ground, being stored, and flowing through waterways. Healthy forests, wetlands and soils help regulate this movement.

RainfallWater enters the ecosystem
→
RunoffWater flows over the land
↔
InfiltrationWater soaks into soil
→
StorageWater is held in soil, wetlands and waterways

Key science words

Runoff – water flowing over the land surface.

Infiltration – water entering and moving into soil.

Erosion – soil is worn away and transported by water or wind.

Nature's water helpers

  • Forests: roots hold soil, vegetation slows runoff, leaf litter protects soil and roots create pathways for water to enter.
  • Wetlands: slow water, store excess water, trap sediment and provide habitats.
  • Healthy soils: allow infiltration, store water for plants, reduce runoff and help reduce erosion.

Remember: these natural features regulate water movement — they do not stop all runoff or erosion.

How can human activity disrupt waterways?

Explore each example. Notice the chain from the human activity, to a change in water movement or quality, to an effect on living things.

Impact 1

Drought

Less water is available for plants and animals. Habitats may shrink and food sources become limited.

Water availability decreases
Impact 2

Wetland drainage

Water storage and wetland habitats are lost. Water may move downstream faster and biodiversity can decrease.

Storage and habitat are lost
Impact 3

Land clearing

Fewer roots hold soil together. Runoff and erosion can increase, carrying sediment into streams.

Runoff and erosion can increase
Impact 4

Pollution

Fertilisers, animal waste and sediment can enter waterways. Water quality declines and aquatic organisms may be harmed.

Water quality declines

Use linking words in your presentation: because · therefore · this can lead to · as a result.

Check your understanding: why might a stream become muddy after nearby trees are removed?

Fewer roots hold the soil together and there is less vegetation to slow runoff. Rain can wash more soil away; sediment is carried into the stream, making the water muddy and potentially damaging aquatic habitats.

What can people do to restore waterways?

Restoration works best when people repair damage, prevent further harm and keep caring for the ecosystem over time.

Approach 1

Restoration

  • Replant native trees and grasses beside streams.
  • Restore drained wetlands.
  • Re-establish native forest.

How it helps: roots hold soil, plants slow runoff, and wetlands store water and trap sediment.

Approach 2

Conservation

  • Protect native forests.
  • Create or maintain predator-free sanctuaries.
  • Limit development in sensitive habitats.

How it helps: protects ecosystems from further damage.

Approach 3

Long-term management

  • Monitor water quality.
  • Control invasive species.
  • Replant where vegetation has failed.

How it helps: checks progress and responds when problems remain.

Kaitiakitanga — ongoing care

Kaitiakitanga is a responsibility to care for the natural environment for current and future generations.

  • Protect waterways and freshwater habitats.
  • Restore native forests and wetlands.
  • Monitor the health of local ecosystems.
  • Work together as a community, including whānau.
  • Take ongoing responsibility — not just complete a one-off activity.

Think about a local stream, wetland or green space: who should be involved, and how could the community contribute?

An example restoration plan

A school and community group want to improve a stream running through farmland. Their proposal is to:

  • Plant native trees and grasses along stream banks.
  • Fence livestock out of the stream.
  • Restore a small wetland beside the stream.
  • Monitor water quality and native vegetation over two years.
Expected benefits

Roots help hold soil together; plants slow runoff; wetlands store water and trap sediment; fencing reduces direct livestock access and contamination; improved habitat may support greater biodiversity.

Why combine actions?

Different actions address different pressures. Monitoring over time helps the community check whether the changes are working.

Evidence from real restoration projects

Scientists check whether restoration is working by measuring changes in waterways and habitats. These New Zealand projects provide examples of evidence you can use in your presentation.

What counts as evidence of success?

Evidence to look forWhat it might show
Less fine sediment or clearer waterLess soil is being washed into the waterway.
Lower nutrient or bacteria levelsWater quality may be improving.
More native plants surviving and growingStream banks and riparian habitats are recovering.
More native fish or sensitive aquatic insectsHabitat conditions may be supporting more freshwater life.

New Zealand restoration examples

Auckland · Mahurangi catchment

Riparian planting and stock exclusion

Researchers monitored nine headwater streams during 2023–2025, comparing open pasture streams with fenced streams planted in native scrub and with mature forest streams.

Evidence of improvement: restored streams had about 32% less fine sediment than open-pasture streams. Sensitive mayflies and caddisflies were found in restored streams, while only a few caddisfly groups were found in open pasture.

This suggests that fencing stock out and planting native vegetation can reduce sediment and improve stream habitat. The study found a relationship; it cannot prove that planting alone caused every difference.

Bay of Plenty · Waitao Stream

Community riparian restoration

Since 2003, local hapū, landowners and community groups have progressively fenced stream margins and planted native vegetation. Water quality, aquatic insects and fish have been monitored at multiple sites.

Evidence of improvement: monitoring showed declining nitrogen and phosphorus, improved water clarity near the stream outlet, and increasing aquatic-insect community scores at some headwater sites. Some of those increases were directly associated with community fencing and planting.

This example shows why repeated monitoring over many years is useful when evaluating restoration.

Waikato · Hakarimata Streams

Native planting for fish habitat

A long-term restoration project used native riparian planting along stream banks to improve habitat for freshwater fish.

Evidence of improvement: the Department of Conservation reports that fish populations increased after the riverbanks were restored through native planting over a 20-year project.

This is an example of a long-term response: habitat restoration can take years before changes in wildlife populations are detected.

Evidence

Information collected through observations or measurements.

Uncertainty

How confident we are that measurements or conclusions are accurate.

Limitations

Features of a study that restrict what we can conclude.

Look beyond the numbers

The number of trees planted or kilometres fenced tells us what work was completed. Changes in sediment, water quality, habitat or species provide evidence about whether the ecosystem is improving.

Think critically: other factors such as rainfall, stream flow, seasons, soil type and upstream land use can also affect results. Stronger evidence comes from using several indicators, repeating measurements over time and comparing sites where possible.
Why take measurements before and after restoration?

Before measurements provide a baseline — a picture of conditions before action begins. After measurements allow scientists to compare conditions and identify changes over time. A comparison site can help scientists decide whether restoration is likely to have made a difference. It is also important to check whether the plants survive and whether maintenance is needed.

Your presentation challenge

Your role

You are an ecological advisor!

Create a presentation explaining how humans can work to restore waterways. Use the science information on this webpage and research evidence from real restoration projects to make a justified recommendation.

Suggested presentation structure

  1. Introduce the issue: explain one human activity that has changed a waterway or catchment.
  2. Explain the science: describe what happens to runoff, infiltration, water storage, erosion or water quality — then link this to biodiversity.
  3. Recommend restoration: describe at least two actions people could take and explain how each action could help.
  4. Use evidence: find evidence of restoration efforts being successful in other projects or places (for example, successful riparian planting). Name the project and location, describe what people did, and include a specific result that shows what improved.
  5. Evaluate: identify one limitation or uncertainty in the evidence. Consider whether other factors could have affected the results or whether more monitoring is needed.
  6. Connect to kaitiakitanga: explain how people can continue caring for the waterway over time.

I recommend that the community plants native vegetation along stream banks and fences livestock out of the waterway. Plant roots help hold soil together, reducing erosion and the amount of sediment entering the stream. Vegetation can also slow runoff and provide habitat for freshwater species.

Evidence from the Mahurangi catchment study supports this approach. Researchers found that streams with fencing and native planting had about 32% less fine sediment than open-pasture streams. Sensitive mayflies and caddisflies were also found in restored streams. This suggests that riparian restoration can improve stream habitat.

However, the study compared different streams, so it cannot prove that planting alone caused every difference. The community should continue monitoring water quality, sediment and freshwater species over time to check whether improvements continue.
